Chapter 3 · Working safely

My AI headquarters: a safe home base for working with Claude

18 November 2026

Before building anything clever with AI, I built the place it works from: a private repo with clear rules, real guardrails and a phone-friendly workflow.

In short

  • What: a private home base where an AI agent works for me under clear rules.
  • How: every change arrives as a pull request, and nothing goes live until I merge it.
  • Surprise: my own tests all passed, but an independent AI reviewer still found nine gaps in my safety guard.

What I asked Claude

I wanted to use AI every day, from my phone, without my laptop on. But before letting an AI agent loose on my files, I asked Claude to help me build a headquarters first. Roughly:

Set up a private repository that holds my rules, notes, plans and prompts. Never do anything I didn’t agree to. Warn me before you get any new access. Every change goes through a pull request that I approve, and you never publish anything without me.

How it works

Think of it as an office with a rulebook and a locked door.

  • The rulebook. Every session, the AI reads a file of rules first: plan before building, work in small steps, check your own work, explain things in plain words, and mark how confident you are. Because each session starts with a blank memory, everything important lives in files, not in chat.
  • The workflow. I describe a task from my phone. The AI works on a separate branch and opens a pull request. I read a short summary, look at the changes, and tap merge if I’m happy. Nothing reaches the main version without me.
  • The locked door. Written rules depend on the AI following them, so I added a hook: a small script that runs automatically before every command, a bit like a database trigger. It blocks the common ways an agent could push to the main branch, force push or merge. It’s a safety net, not a wall: a determined workaround could still get past it, which is why the main branch also gets GitHub’s own protection once the site is public.
  • A second pair of eyes. For important work, a separate AI reviewer that didn’t build the thing checks it against my rules and tells me what’s risky or missing.

My mistake

A few days after I built the guard, I opened a session with two repositories in it. The guard quietly stopped loading, and nothing told me. I only found out because I tested it. Everything I thought was locked had been open the whole time.

The rule it gave me: every session starts with a test push to the main branch, and it must be blocked. If it isn’t, all other work stops until the guard is back.

What I learned

  • Rules are a sign on the door. Hooks are the lock. If something really matters, enforce it, don’t just write it down.
  • My own tests weren’t enough. The guard passed every test I wrote, and then an independent reviewer found nine gaps in it, including one way around it that I’d never have thought of. Fixing them taught me more than building it did!
  • Least access is a habit, not a setting. Each new permission now comes with a short warning: what it unlocks, what could go wrong, and how to undo it.
  • Slow at the start makes everything after it faster. With the base in place, every new project starts from rules and guardrails I already trust.

Try it yourself

Paste this at the start of your next chat with an AI agent:

Before you change anything, write a short plan and wait for my OK.
Work in small steps, and check your own work after each one.
Explain what you did in plain words, and tell me how confident you are.
Never publish, delete or push anything without asking me first.

It takes ten seconds. The agent gets a little slower to act, and a lot easier to trust.
